# Possible causes and associated risk factors

The exact cause of PCOS is not known. Research points to a combination of inherited and biological influences rather than one behavior or one food. PCOS can occur in people at different body sizes; it should not be treated as a personal failure or assumed to be caused by weight. [NICHD: PCOS](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/pcos) · [ACOG: PCOS](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/polycystic-ovary-syndrome-pcos)

Insulin resistance and higher androgen levels are often associated with PCOS and can interact with ovulation and symptoms. These are clinical concepts, not something that can be established from a chat or a screening score. Family history may also be relevant. A clinician can decide whether assessment for blood sugar, cholesterol, hormones, or other conditions is appropriate. [ACOG: PCOS](https://www.acog.org/womens-health/faqs/polycystic-ovary-syndrome-pcos) · [CDC: PCOS and diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/risk-factors/pcos-polycystic-ovary-syndrome.html)

PCOS is associated with increased likelihood of some health concerns, including type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, abnormal cholesterol, sleep apnea, and anxiety or depression. These are risks at a population level, not predictions for a particular person. Regular care can help a clinician identify which checks fit an individual's health. [CDC: PCOS and diabetes](https://www.cdc.gov/diabetes/risk-factors/pcos-polycystic-ovary-syndrome.html) · [NICHD: PCOS](https://www.nichd.nih.gov/health/topics/pcos)

This is general screening education, not a diagnosis or individualized risk assessment. Please discuss personal risks with a doctor.
